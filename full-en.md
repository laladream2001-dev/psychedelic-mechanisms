# Hallucinogenic Mechanisms of Psychedelics: From Molecular Pharmacology to Predictive Processing
A Comprehensive Monograph on Classical Serotonergic Psychedelics, Dissociative NMDA Antagonists, and Atypical Opioid Hallucinogens
## PREFACE & SCOPE
The scientific understanding of altered states of consciousness induced by psychoactive substances has undergone a paradigm shift over the past two decades. Historically categorized under broad, descriptive terms, modern neuropharmacology and computational neuroscience now differentiate these agents into distinct functional and mechanistically defined classes. This monograph presents a multi-level synthesis spanning molecular pharmacology, intracellular signaling cascades, structural and functional neuroplasticity, large-scale neural network dynamics, and theoretical/computational models of brain function (specifically hierarchical predictive processing and active inference).
The scope encompasses three primary classes of hallucinogenic and psychoactive compounds:
Classical Serotonergic Psychedelics: Tryptamines (e.g., psilocybin, DMT), ergolines (e.g., LSD), and phenethylamines (e.g., mescaline, 2C-B, 25I-NBOMe) acting principally via 5-HT2A receptor agonism.
Dissociative Anesthetics: Use-dependent, uncompetitive N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) open-channel blockers (e.g., ketamine, PCP).
Atypical Hallucinogens: Kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) agonists (e.g., salvinorin A) and polypharmacological indole alkaloids (e.g., ibogaine).
By bridging sub-cellular signaling with system-level network alterations, this work aims to provide a unified framework for how structurally disparate molecules modulate perception, self-referential cognition, and neural plasticity.

## CHAPTER 1: TAXONOMY AND CHEMICAL ARCHITECTURE OF HALLUCINOGENS
The academic investigation of hallucinogenic agents has evolved significantly since psychiatrist Humphry Osmond coined the term "psychedelic" (mind-manifesting) in 1956 (Osmond, 1957), and since Albert Hofmann's synthesis of lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) in 1938, followed by his accidental exposure on 16 April 1943 and intentional use on 19 April 1943 (as reviewed in the secondary historical account by McCorvy & Roth, 2015). Early clinical interest waned following mid-20th-century regulatory restrictions, but contemporary high-resolution neuroimaging, structural biology, and molecular signaling assays have catalyzed a renaissance in translational psychedelic science (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Modern chemical taxonomy classifies hallucinogens based on primary molecular scaffolds, pharmacophores, and core target binding profiles.
Schematic Summary (Chemical Classification): Hallucinogenic agents are organized into three primary classes: (1) classical serotonergic psychedelics—tryptamines (psilocybin, DMT), ergolines (LSD), and phenethylamines (mescaline, 25I-NBOMe); (2) classical dissociative anesthetics acting as use-dependent, uncompetitive NMDAR open-channel blockers (ketamine, PCP); and (3) atypical hallucinogens—neoclerodane diterpenes (salvinorin A at KOR) and polypharmacological indole alkaloids (ibogaine).
### Classical Serotonergic Psychedelics
Classical psychedelics share structural homology with the endogenous neurotransmitter serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) and belong to three principal chemical classes:
Tryptamines: Characterized by a core indole ring connected to an amino group via a two-carbon ethyl chain. Representative agents include psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, a prodrug rapidly dephosphorylated in vivo to the active metabolite psilocin), N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT) (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Ergolines: Complex tetracyclic compounds built on a rigid tetracyclic ergoline scaffold containing both indole and phenethylamine motifs. Lysergamides represent a subclass of ergolines defined by a lysergic acid amide functionality rather than defining the ergoline class as a whole (McCorvy & Roth, 2015). LSD is the canonical psychedelic lysergamide (an ergoline derivative), exhibiting high affinity across serotonin, dopamine, and adrenergic receptor subtypes (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Phenethylamines: Composed of a benzene ring linked to an amino group by an ethyl chain. Key members include naturally occurring mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) and synthetic modifications such as 2C-B (4-bromo-2,5-dimethoxyphenethylamine), DOI (4-iodo-2,5-dimethoxyamphetamine), DOM, and the high-affinity N-(2-methoxybenzyl) derivatives such as 25I-NBOMe (Banushi & Polito, 2023; Ganado et al., 2026).
### Dissociative Anesthetics
Classical dissociative anesthetics such as ketamine (specifically its (S)- and (R)-enantiomers) and phencyclidine (PCP) disrupt sensory transmission and conscious awareness primarily through use-dependent, uncompetitive open-channel blockade of the ionotropic N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) channel complex (MacDonald et al., 1991; Marguilho et al., 2023). Although gaseous nitrous oxide (N2O) is also an inhalational anesthetic that inhibits postsynaptic NMDAR function, electrophysiological evidence argues against classifying it as a classical ketamine-like, use-dependent open-channel blocker: N2O inhibition develops rapidly without altering NMDAR excitatory current decay kinetics and appears independent of channel opening (Mennerick et al., 1998).
### Atypical Hallucinogens
Compounds outside classical serotonergic or NMDAR-antagonist categories produce distinct hallucinogenic profiles:
Salvinorin A: A non-nitrogenous neoclerodane diterpene isolated from Salvia divinorum. Unlike virtually all other known hallucinogens, it lacks an amine nitrogen and acts as a selective agonist at the κ-opioid receptor (KOR) relative to other major opioid receptor subtypes and 5-HT2AR within screened binding panels (Butelman & Kreek, 2015).
Ibogaine: A monoterpene indole alkaloid extracted from Tabernanthe iboga. Ibogaine features a polypharmacological profile, interacting with NMDARs, KORs, μ-opioid receptors (MOR), 5-HT receptors, and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α3β4 nAChR) (Ganado et al., 2026).
## CHAPTER 2: CLASSICAL SEROTONERGIC PSYCHEDELICS — RECEPTOR PHARMACOLOGY AND INTRACELLULAR SIGNALING
### Centrality of the 5-HT2A Receptor
The primary mechanism underlying the hallucinogenic properties of classical psychedelics is partial or full agonism at the serotonin 2A receptor (5-HT2AR), a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Banushi & Polito, 2023). While 5-HT2AR binding affinity is broadly associated with human hallucinogenic potency—though receptor binding affinity alone does not determine potency—human challenge studies using pre-administration of the 5-HT2AR antagonist ketanserin provide compound-specific evidence rather than a single uniform demonstration across agents; notably, ketanserin is not exclusively selective for 5-HT2AR (exhibiting 5-HT2A/2C antagonism; Knight et al., 2004), whereas more selective 5-HT2AR antagonists such as M100907 (MDL 100,907, exhibiting greater than approximately 100-fold selectivity for 5-HT2A over other measured receptors—including 5-HT2C—in the cited binding panels, though exact fold-selectivity values depend on the specific assay panel used) exist (Kehne et al., 1996; Knight et al., 2004). Specifically, Preller et al. (2019) directly tested LSD in humans (showing ketanserin blockade of acute subjective effects and 5-HT2AR-dependent thalamus–PCC effective connectivity changes, though not all connectivity alterations); Vollenweider et al. (1998) evaluated ketanserin pretreatment against psilocybin in healthy human volunteers, demonstrating dose-dependent blockade of psilocybin-induced effects; and human ketanserin evidence relating to DMT involves ayahuasca—a brew combining DMT with β-carboline MAO inhibitors rather than pure DMT alone—in which oral ketanserin (40 mg; n = 12) inhibited EEG alpha modifications and attenuated rather than completely abolished acute subjective effects of ayahuasca (0.75 mg DMT/kg) (Valle et al., 2016).
5-HT2A receptors are expressed most densely on the apical dendrites of layer 5 pyramidal neurons within the neocortex, particularly within high-level association areas (such as the default mode network nodes) and primary/extrastriate visual cortices (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Schematic Summary (5-HT2AR Intracellular Signaling): Ligand binding at 5-HT2AR engages two reviewed intracellular signaling branches (Banushi & Polito, 2023): (1) the canonical Gq/11 pathway activating PLCβ (correcting Banushi & Polito's erroneous reference to PLCγ) to cleave PIP2 into IP3 (mobilizing intracellular Ca2+) and DAG (activating PKC), converging on ERK1/2 and CREB modulation; and (2) PI3K/AKT signaling.
### Functional Selectivity and Biased Agonism
Non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A agonists can stimulate the receptor without reliably inducing hallucinogenic behavioral outputs (e.g., the head-twitch response [HTR] in rodents), whereas the case of serotonin is distinct and conditional: peripheral/systemic serotonin administration is not equivalent to delivering serotonin into the brain or to intracellular 5-HT2AR pools, and serotonin or serotonin-releasing manipulations can induce HTR under appropriate conditions—indeed, Vargas et al. (2023) reported HTR following a serotonin-releasing agent in mice engineered to express the serotonin transporter (SERT) in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Functional selectivity (biased agonism) has been proposed as one model that may explain ligand divergence at 5-HT2AR, alongside location bias, pharmacokinetics, and signaling kinetics (Banushi & Polito, 2023; Vargas et al., 2023), while Vargas et al. (2023) note that traditional biased agonism alone does not explain serotonin's growth phenotype.
Upon ligand binding, GPCRs adopt distinct active conformations that preferentially recruit specific intracellular transducers:
Gq/11 Activation: Canonical binding triggers Gq/11-mediated activation of Phospholipase C beta (PLCβ)—noting that Banushi & Polito (2023) erroneously name PLCγ here, whereas PLCβ is the canonical Gq/11 effector—leading to the cleavage of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). IP3 mobilizes intracellular calcium (Ca2+) from the endoplasmic reticulum, while DAG activates Protein Kinase C (PKC), downstream Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK1/2), and Cyclic AMP Response Element-Binding Protein (CREB) (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
PI3K/AKT Signaling: In addition to canonical Gq/11 signaling, 5-HT2AR activation engages Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and AKT signaling cascades (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
At the local network level, 5-HT2AR activation on layer 5 pyramidal neurons promotes asynchronous glutamate release from thalamocortical and corticocortical terminals, generating spike-field decoherence and altering local recurrent excitation (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
### Location Bias: Intracellular 5-HT2A Receptors
A critical paradox in neuropharmacology is why endogenous serotonin does not trigger the same structural plastic changes as lipophilic psychedelics despite possessing high affinity for 5-HT2AR. In preclinical neuronal models, Vargas et al. (2023) proposed that location bias accounts for this divergence in structural neuroplasticity.
A substantial pool of 5-HT2A receptors resides inside cortical neurons within the Golgi apparatus and endosomal membranes, whereas embryonic rat cortical neurons do not naturally express SERT at appreciable levels (Vargas et al., 2023). To test whether intracellular access enables serotonin-driven plasticity, Vargas et al. (2023) electroporated embryonic rat cortical neurons with a SERT construct under a CaMKII promoter, generating engineered SERT-positive alongside SERT-negative neurons. Serotonin promoted dendritic growth in these engineered SERT-positive neurons—an effect on the dendrite-growth/Sholl endpoint (Figure 4D) that was blocked by citalopram or ketanserin—whereas unmodified/SERT-negative neurons did not show this response; spine growth in SERT-positive neurons was assessed separately (Figure 4E–F) rather than through the same direct inhibitor comparison (Vargas et al., 2023). Similarly, their in vivo experiments relied on viral SERT expression in the mouse mPFC followed by a serotonin-releasing agent, and therefore do not demonstrate that normal endogenous serotonin accesses intracellular 5-HT2AR pools in unmodified cortical pyramidal neurons (Vargas et al., 2023). Conversely, more lipophilic psychedelic molecules (such as DMT, psilocin, and LSD) more readily cross the plasma membrane to engage intracellular 5-HT2AR populations (Vargas et al., 2023). Across the preclinical models tested by Vargas et al. (2023), the directly measured endpoints encompassed dendritic branching, dendritic spine density, and electrophysiological changes (including spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents [sEPSCs])—collectively interpreted cautiously as structural neuroplasticity—but receptor dependence and intracellular access were demonstrated in distinct experiments: 5-HT2AR knockout prevented the sustained sEPSC changes after 5-MeO-DMT in mice (demonstrating receptor dependence rather than specifically proving intracellular receptor necessity; Supplementary Figure S1C); separate membrane-permeability experiments addressed intracellular access for structural endpoints such as dendritic branching and spine density (Figures 3–5). Furthermore, as Vargas et al. (2023) note in their Discussion, their experiments support intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement but do not distinguish intracellular signaling from alternative hypotheses such as pharmacological chaperoning; thus, the underlying mechanism (intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning) remains unresolved, and whether intracellular 5-HT2AR engagement is sufficient to explain neuroplasticity in humans has not been demonstrated (Vargas et al., 2023).
### Direct TrkB Binding and Neuroplasticity
Beyond traditional GPCR activation, direct engagement of neurotrophin signaling pathways has also been proposed, although this mechanism remains contested. In preclinical molecular and cellular models, Moliner et al. (2023) reported that LSD and psilocin bind directly to Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), the primary receptor for Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) (Moliner et al., 2023).
In that study, LSD and psilocin were reported to bind to the dimeric transmembrane domain of TrkB with high binding affinity—measured in vitro using immunoprecipitated TrkB expressed in a heterologous HEK293T cell system (a heterologous in vitro receptor preparation rather than native human brain tissue) and described as an approximate, rounded ~1,000-fold higher affinity relative to comparator antidepressants (for example, psilocin Ki = 6.73 nM versus fluoxetine Ki = 4.77 μM [~700-fold] and ketamine Ki = 20.82 μM [~3,100-fold]), rather than an exact 1,000-fold measurement or 1,000-fold signal amplification (Moliner et al., 2023). According to Moliner et al. (2023), this binding stabilizes the active dimeric conformation of TrkB, facilitating endogenous BDNF signaling, dendritic arborization, and spinogenesis (Moliner et al., 2023). In their binding-site characterization experiments, both the Y433F and V437A point mutations in the TrkB transmembrane domain markedly reduced binding, while a separate manipulation replacing the TrkB transmembrane domain with the TrkA domain resulted in loss of binding in radioligand assays; however, the dissociation between neuroplasticity and behavior in mice—loss of neuroplastic effects without preventing the 5-HT2AR-mediated head-twitch response, a behavioral marker rather than a direct measure of hallucinogenic subjective effects—was specifically established with the Y433F system, including heterozygous Y433F mice, which the authors interpreted as evidence for a 5-HT2AR-independent dissociation between this behavioral marker and sustained neuroplasticity via BDNF-dependent allosteric potentiation (Moliner et al., 2023). However, these findings have been challenged by subsequent profiling: in a comprehensive study profiling 41 psychedelics across 318 human GPCRs, Jain et al. (2025) screened LSD against over 450 human kinases (including the intracellular kinase domain of TrkB) and found no significant interaction, and in a functional TrkB AP1-luciferase reporter assay in TrkB-transfected cells specifically comparing LSD and psilocin, neither compound affected BDNF-driven TrkB reporter activation (assessing downstream transcriptional AP1-luciferase signaling under transfected-cell conditions rather than directly probing transmembrane-domain binding; Jain et al., 2025). While the kinase-binding screen does not directly test Moliner et al.'s (2023) proposed transmembrane binding site and therefore does not by itself refute binding at that site, the failure to detect modulation of BDNF-driven TrkB activity in the AP1-luciferase reporter assay directly bears on Moliner et al.'s (2023) explicitly proposed functional mechanism of BDNF-dependent allosteric potentiation; overall, the assays differ, the evidence remains conflicting and inconclusive, and direct TrkB engagement by psychedelics remains an ongoing debate (Moliner et al., 2023; Jain et al., 2025). Moreover, Moliner et al.'s (2023) proposal of 5-HT2AR-independent plasticity stands in direct tension with Vargas et al. (2023), who reported that intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement (with intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning unresolved) drives structural neuroplasticity in their preclinical models, rather than representing cleanly complementary mechanisms.
### Secondary Receptor Targets
Classical psychedelics interact with a broad spectrum of secondary targets:
5-HT1A and 5-HT2C Receptors: In addition to 5-HT2AR, classical psychedelics interact with secondary serotonin receptor subtypes, including 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C receptors (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Dopamine D2 Receptors: LSD displays high affinity for dopamine D2 receptors; in rat behavioral models, it produces a characteristically biphasic behavioral effect wherein late-stage behavioral dynamics are mediated by D2R engagement (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Trace Amine-Associated Receptor 1 (TAAR1): Some tryptamines interact with TAAR1 in experimental assays; however, receptor activity observed in experimental systems must be distinguished from established in vivo functional consequences, and the contribution of this interaction to DMT's psychedelic effects is unresolved (Su et al., 2009; Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Sigma-1 Receptors (σ1R): DMT acts as a ligand at σ1R, localized at the mitochondrion-associated endoplasmic reticulum membrane (MAM), with an in vitro binding affinity described as "about 14 μM" in Su et al. (2009, reviewing Fontanilla et al., 2009) and cited as "14 μM" in the theoretical model in the caption of Figure 3 of Banushi & Polito (2023); importantly, this value reflects in vitro binding affinity rather than an endogenous concentration in the brain. Within the theoretical framework reviewed by Su et al. (2009) and Banushi & Polito (2023), σ1R actions are separated into two distinct concentration branches: (a) at concentrations near binding affinity, proposed σ1R/BiP dissociation and IP3-receptor chaperoning, along with proposed modulation of endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer and ATP production; and (b) at higher concentrations, illustrated by ~100 μM DMT, proposed receptor redistribution alongside experimentally observed in vitro ion-channel inhibition (Fontanilla et al., 2009). Crucially, proposed mechanisms must be distinguished from experimentally observed findings: the DMT chaperone/transport scheme (σ1R/BiP dissociation, IP3-receptor chaperoning, endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, ATP production, and proposed cellular protection against oxidative stress) is a hypothesis extrapolated from general σ1R biology and other σ1R ligands, whereas the in vitro ion-channel experiments with DMT do not demonstrate that those same preparations exhibit DMT-induced mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, increased ATP production, or protection against oxidative stress—and none of these proposed pathways has been established in the human brain (Fontanilla et al., 2009; Su et al., 2009; Banushi & Polito, 2023).
## CHAPTER 3: SYSTEMS NEUROSCIENCE OF CLASSICAL PSYCHEDELICS — CSTC, CCC, DMN, AND REBUS
### Cortico-Striato-Thalamo-Cortical (CSTC) Loop Gating
The Cortico-Striato-Thalamo-Cortical (CSTC) model is a theoretical/systems-level framework positing that the thalamus serves as an activity-dependent filter regulating feedforward sensory and interoceptive information flow to the cerebral cortex (Preller et al., 2019; Kwan et al., 2022).
Schematic Summary (CSTC Loop Gating): In the theoretical CSTC model, cortical layer 5 pyramidal neurons project through basal ganglia inhibitory output pathways (via striatum and pallidal output nuclei such as GPi) to regulate gating over the relay thalamus, modulating feedforward sensory flow to the cortex (Doss et al., 2022; Kwan et al., 2022). Empirically in humans under LSD (n = 25), Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) effective connectivity from the thalamus to the PCC increases in a ketanserin-sensitive, 5-HT2A-dependent manner, whereas positive/excitatory ventral striatum-to-thalamus DCM effective connectivity decreases independently of ketanserin blockade (Preller et al., 2019).
Within this theoretical model, 5-HT2AR stimulation on cortical L5 and striatal neurons has been hypothesized to alter basal ganglia inhibitory output pathways to the thalamus, loosening the "thalamic filter" (Doss et al., 2022; Kwan et al., 2022). Importantly, this anatomical basal ganglia inhibitory output pathway must be distinguished from Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) effective connectivity estimated between selected regional BOLD signals: in Preller et al.'s (2019) DCM model across 25 human participants, ventral striatum-to-thalamus effective connectivity carries a positive (excitatory) sign, so a decrease in that model parameter is not itself a measurement of a direct inhibitory striato-thalamic projection or of reduced GABA release. Empirically, Preller et al. (2019) demonstrated a pharmacological dissociation between the two modeled CSTC branches under LSD: increased thalamus-to-posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) effective connectivity was ketanserin-sensitive (dependent on 5-HT2A receptor activation), whereas decreased ventral striatum-to-thalamus effective connectivity was ketanserin-insensitive (independent of 5-HT2A receptor blockade) (Preller et al., 2019; Kwan et al., 2022). Separately from Preller et al. (2019), deficits in Prepulse Inhibition (PPI) of the acoustic startle reflex have been discussed in the broader CSTC literature as a behavioral index of altered sensorimotor gating, though PPI was not assessed in Preller et al.'s effective connectivity study.
### Claustro-Cortical Circuit (CCC) Model
The claustrum—a thin, highly interconnected subcortical nucleus—expresses abundant 5-HT2A receptors, forming the basis of the cortico-claustro-cortical (CCC) model of psychedelic action (Barrett et al., 2020; Doss et al., 2022). In human functional neuroimaging, psilocybin altered functional connectivity of the right claustrum bidirectionally: it decreased functional connectivity between the right claustrum and both the auditory network and the Default Mode Network (DMN), while increasing functional connectivity between the right claustrum and the frontoparietal task control network (Barrett et al., 2020). Regarding subjective effects, Barrett et al. (2020) reported exploratory associations involving claustrum BOLD signal amplitude/variance and ratings such as overall drug intensity, mystical experience, and ineffability, rather than showing that these three specific right-claustrum connectivity changes correlated with ego dissolution or cognitive reorganization; moreover, those exploratory subjective associations did not remain statistically significant after multiple-comparison correction (Barrett et al., 2020).
### Default Mode Network, Alpha Oscillations, and the Entropic Brain
Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) demonstrate that classical psychedelics disrupt established large-scale brain networks (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019):
DMN Disintegration: The Default Mode Network (comprising the PCC, medial prefrontal cortex [mPFC], and angular gyrus) exhibits marked within-network functional desynchronization under psilocybin and LSD (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Global Integration: Concurrently, localized functional network boundaries undergo functional desegregation, yielding an increase in cross-network functional connectivity (e.g., functional coupling between visual cortex and auditory and somatosensory networks, without implying new anatomical connections) (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Oscillatory Suppression: MEG studies reveal dramatic, broadband reductions in spontaneous oscillatory power, most prominently in the alpha frequency band within the PCC (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). The magnitude of alpha power suppression within the PCC is associated with subjective ratings of ego dissolution (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Entropic Brain Hypothesis: In this theoretical model, psychedelics elevate Lempel-Ziv signal complexity in cortical electrophysiological recordings, shifting macro-scale neural dynamics away from rigid, constrained regimes toward a state characterized by increased informational entropy and stronger signatures of criticality (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
### REBUS and the Anarchic Brain
The REBUS (RElaxed Beliefs Under pSychedelics) model is a theoretical framework that integrates the Entropic Brain Hypothesis with hierarchical Bayesian predictive processing and the Free-Energy Principle (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Schematic Summary (REBUS Hierarchical Predictive Processing): Within this theoretical framework, high-level priors (DMN/mPFC, with deep-layer L5 pyramidal neurons hypothesized to encode posterior expectations/priors) send top-down predictions downward across cortical layers, while unconstrained afferent sensory and interoceptive streams send bottom-up prediction errors surging upward when high-level prior weighting is relaxed via 5-HT2AR agonism (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
In the theoretical framework of hierarchical predictive coding, the brain generates top-down predictions (priors) that constrain and interpret bottom-up sensory information (prediction errors). Deep-layer cortical L5 pyramidal neurons are hypothesized to encode posterior expectations (priors), rather than the precision weighting of those priors (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Within this theoretical model, by directly activating 5-HT2AR on L5 neurons, classical psychedelics desynchronize deep-layer activity, selectively decreasing the precision weighting of high-level priors (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). This process "flattens" the free-energy landscape (analogous to simulated annealing in optimization algorithms). As high-level cognitive constraints relax:
Unconstrained bottom-up sensory and emotional prediction errors cascade up the hierarchy into conscious awareness, causing visual distortions and unprompted memory surfacing (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Rigid, pathological high-level priors (e.g., negative self-schemas in major depressive disorder or compulsive loops in OCD) become malleable, theoretically allowing Bayesian Model Reduction (BMR) to reorganize belief architectures upon acute drug clearance (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
## CHAPTER 4: DISSOCIATIVE HALLUCINOGENS — KETAMINE AND NMDA RECEPTOR ANTAGONISM
### Molecular Mechanisms & Dual Hypotheses
Ketamine operates primarily as a use-dependent, uncompetitive open-channel antagonist of the ionotropic N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), binding to the phencyclidine (PCP) site within the open channel pore (MacDonald et al., 1991; Marguilho et al., 2023). In addition, recent structural evidence demonstrates that ketamine and PCP can directly bind and activate human opioid receptors (including MOR and KOR), although the extent to which opioid receptor engagement contributes to ketamine's distinct clinical effects remains an emerging finding requiring further evaluation (Jiang et al., 2026). Two main cellular hypotheses—investigated primarily in preclinical models—have been proposed mainly to account for ketamine's rapid antidepressant and neuroplasticity-related effects rather than its acute dissociative phenomenology (Marguilho et al., 2023). Specifically, the eEF2–BDNF direct-inhibition pathway has not been demonstrated to explain acute dissociation; instead, Marguilho et al. (2023) separately propose network-level explanations for ketamine's acute dissociative and psychedelic-like phenomena (discussed below):
Schematic Summary (Preclinical Ketamine Cellular Hypotheses for Antidepressant/Plasticity-Related Effects): Two principal preclinical pathways proposed for rapid antidepressant and plasticity-related effects converge on BDNF signaling and mTORC1-associated synaptogenesis, without explaining acute dissociation (Marguilho et al., 2023): (1) the Disinhibition Hypothesis—blockade of NMDARs on GABAergic (SST/PV) interneurons reduces cortical inhibition, triggering a glutamate surge, postsynaptic AMPAR activation, Ca2+ influx via VGCCs, and activity-dependent BDNF release; and (2) the Direct Inhibition Hypothesis—blockade of tonic resting-state NMDARs on pyramidal neurons suppresses eEF2K, promoting eEF2 dephosphorylation and rapid dendritic BDNF translation.
Disinhibition Hypothesis: Low sub-anesthetic doses of ketamine selectively block NMDARs present on tonically active γ-aminobutyric acid (GABAergic) interneurons (e.g., somatostatin- and parvalbumin-expressing interneurons) (Marguilho et al., 2023). This disinhibits cortical excitatory pyramidal neurons, causing a transient surge in extracellular glutamate. The glutamate surge activates postsynaptic α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs), driving depolarization, entry of Ca2+ through voltage-gated channels, activity-dependent release of BDNF, and activation of the mTOR Complex 1 (mTORC1) cascade (Marguilho et al., 2023).
Direct Inhibition Hypothesis: Direct blockade of resting, spontaneously active postsynaptic NMDARs on pyramidal neurons suppresses eukaryotic elongation factor 2 kinase (eEF2K) activity. Dephosphorylation of eEF2 relieves translation suppression of BDNF, leading to rapid BDNF protein synthesis and downstream dendritic spine consolidation (Marguilho et al., 2023).
### Reward Circuitry and Habenular Bursting
In preclinical rodent models, ketamine targets the lateral habenula (LHb), a subcortical region that acts as an "anti-reward center" by suppressing dopamine release from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) during negative reward prediction errors (Marguilho et al., 2023). Depressive states are characterized by pathological, hyperactive NMDA- and T-type voltage-gated Ca2+ channel-dependent burst firing in LHb neurons (Marguilho et al., 2023). Ketamine rapidly blocks NMDAR-dependent bursting in the LHb. This can disinhibit downstream monoaminergic reward centers and has been proposed to contribute to antidepressant effects (Marguilho et al., 2023).
### Unified Theoretical/Conceptual Model (Salience vs. Default Mode Network)
Unlike 5-HT2A receptors, which concentrate heavily in high-order transmodal association and visual networks, NMDARs exhibit broad (though non-uniform) expression across the cerebral cortex rather than restriction to transmodal hubs. Within this broader cortical distribution compared with 5-HT2ARs, Marguilho et al. (2023) propose a distinct conceptual network impact:
Schematic Summary — Conceptual Model-Based Dose-Dependent Neural Dynamics of Ketamine (Qualitative Conceptual Levels): In Marguilho et al.'s (2023) conceptual framework, ketamine's neural effects are organized across three qualitative exposure levels—lower sub-anesthetic, moderate sub-anesthetic, and anesthetic—without fixed numerical cutoffs, because actual pharmacodynamic thresholds depend critically on species, route, formulation (e.g., racemic vs. enantiomeric), and rate of administration. Within this conceptual framework: (1) the Lower Sub-anesthetic Level may disrupt the Salience Network (SN) and relax high-level bodily/minimal-self priors (distinct from low-level sensory priors); (2) the Moderate Sub-anesthetic Level may expand disruption to the Default Mode Network (DMN), relaxing highest-level narrative/autobiographical priors and potentially inducing ego dissolution; and (3) the Anesthetic Level may induce alternating slow-delta and high-frequency gamma bursts alongside anesthetic unresponsiveness.
Lower Sub-anesthetic Level (conceptual level in Marguilho et al., 2023): Because effective exposures depend on species, route, formulation, and rate of administration, this level is framed qualitatively rather than as a fixed quantitative threshold (Marguilho et al., 2023). In Marguilho et al.'s (2023) conceptual model, ketamine at lower sub-anesthetic exposures may preferentially disrupt the Salience Network (SN; insular and anterior cingulate cortices)—which in their framework encodes high-level priors concerning the body and minimal self (positioned below the narrative self in the cortical hierarchy, rather than representing low-level sensory priors)—relaxing these bodily/minimal-self priors and potentially inducing dissociative depersonalization and derealization (Marguilho et al., 2023).
Moderate Sub-anesthetic Level (conceptual level in Marguilho et al., 2023): Likewise dependent on species, route, formulation, and administration rate rather than defined by a universal numerical cutoff, disruption at moderate sub-anesthetic exposures in this conceptual classification may expand into the Default Mode Network (DMN), relaxing the highest-level narrative and autobiographical priors and potentially inducing psychedelic-like ego dissolution (Marguilho et al., 2023).
Anesthetic Level (conceptual level in Marguilho et al., 2023): Contingent on species, route, formulation, and administration rate rather than a single standardized dose figure, ketamine at anesthetic levels may induce alternating slow-delta oscillations and high-frequency gamma bursts (the "ketamine rhythm"), suppressing large-scale cortical communication and producing an anesthetic state of unresponsiveness (Marguilho et al., 2023).
## CHAPTER 5: ATYPICAL HALLUCINOGENS — KAPPA-OPIOID AGONISM (SALVINORIN A) AND POLYPHARMACOLOGY (IBOGAINE)
### Salvinorin A and Kappa-Opioid Receptor (KOR) Dynamics
Salvinorin A, isolated from Salvia divinorum, is a neoclerodane diterpene lacking a basic nitrogen atom (Butelman & Kreek, 2015). It acts as a potent, selective agonist at the κ-opioid receptor (KOR) (e.g., G-protein activation EC50 = 4.73 nM at mouse KOR; White et al., 2015), with virtually no affinity for 5-HT2AR and selectivity bounded by the receptor panels screened (Butelman & Kreek, 2015).
Schematic Summary (Salvinorin A KOR Signaling): KOR engagement by salvinorin A activates the canonical Gi/o pathway—inhibiting adenylyl cyclase, reducing intracellular cAMP, opening GIRK channels, and inhibiting voltage-gated Ca2+ channels to promote neuronal hyperpolarization (Ganado et al., 2026)—and also directly recruits β-arrestin and drives strong receptor internalization (White et al., 2014; White et al., 2015). A balanced (unbiased) KOR agonist engages both measured G-protein and arrestin pathways roughly equivalently within the tested assay system; accordingly, primary profiling contradicts Ganado et al.'s (2026) claim of pronounced β-arrestin bias, not the existence of β-arrestin recruitment itself, while specific downstream p38 MAPK/JNK effects and proposed links to transient plasticity or phenomenology remain separate questions (White et al., 2014; White et al., 2015; Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026).
At the cellular level, KOR activation by Salvinorin A engages two primary pathways:
Gi/o Signaling: Inhibits adenylyl cyclase, reducing intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels, opening G protein-coupled inwardly rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels, and inhibiting voltage-gated Ca2+ channels. This hyperpolarizes target neurons and inhibits neurotransmitter release (Ganado et al., 2026).
β-Arrestin Recruitment, Relative Pathway Bias, and Downstream Signaling: Interpreting non-G-protein signaling by salvinorin A requires separating four distinct levels of evidence:
(a) Demonstrated arrestin recruitment: A balanced (unbiased) agonist denotes roughly equivalent engagement of the measured pathways within a given assay system—it does not mean an absence of β-arrestin recruitment. Primary pharmacological assays directly measure β-arrestin recruitment by salvinorin A (e.g., arrestin mobilization EC50 = 10.5 nM alongside G-protein activation EC50 = 4.73 nM at mouse KOR) together with strong KOR internalization (White et al., 2014; White et al., 2015).
(b) Relative pathway bias: These primary experiments contradict Ganado et al.'s (2026) characterization of salvinorin A as exhibiting pronounced β-arrestin bias, not the existence of arrestin recruitment itself. In White et al.'s (2015) RB-64 comparison analysis, salvinorin A has a bias factor of 1 because values are normalized to it as the reference ligand within that assay system—which does not establish a universal absence of bias across all pathways, cell types, species, and assay time points, as White et al. (2015) explicitly discuss assay and system limitations—whereas the salvinorin A derivative RB-64 exhibits pronounced G-protein bias at mouse KOR (bias factor = 96 toward G protein; White et al., 2015), consistent with Butelman & Kreek (2015, citing White et al., 2014).
(c) Evidence for specific p38 MAPK / JNK effects: Downstream p38 Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) and c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK) activation and rapid receptor desensitization are attributed to KOR β-arrestin-2 signaling in the review by Ganado et al. (2026), but are distinct from—and not demonstrated as a salvinorin A–specific biased signature by—White et al.'s (2014, 2015) arrestin-recruitment and internalization assays.
(d) Proposed links to plasticity and phenomenology: Proposed connections between these intracellular cascades and transient functional plasticity or dissociative phenomenology remain interpretive; in contrast to classical serotonergic psychedelics, Ganado et al. (2026) contrast salvinorin A's transient functional plasticity with sustained structural spinogenesis, though the cited review provides no direct dendritic-spine imaging evidence for salvinorin A.
### Circuit-Level Mechanisms of Salvinorin A
VTA-Nucleus Accumbens Suppression: KORs are heavily expressed on presynaptic dopaminergic terminals originating in the VTA and projecting to the nucleus accumbens (Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). As summarized in Ganado et al. (2026), rat nucleus accumbens microdialysis studies report a reduction in extracellular dopamine (dialysate) following systemic salvinorin A, a preclinical observation associated with conditioned place aversion in animal models—though Butelman & Kreek (2015) also raise the possibility of increased dopamine transporter (DAT) activity rather than solely reduced dopamine release. However, dopaminergic and behavioral reinforcement responses vary substantially by dose, route, species, and brain region—indicating that preclinical reinforcement evidence is limited and inconsistent, and route- and dose-dependent, rather than showing an absence of rewarding effects: in rats, Braida et al. (2008) reported conditioned place preference and intracerebroventricular self-administration at certain doses (alongside low-dose dopamine increases), and Serra et al. (2015) found increased nucleus accumbens extracellular dopamine (dialysate) without stable intravenous self-administration at the tested doses, whereas Zhang et al. (2005) measured extracellular dopamine (dialysate) reductions in the mouse caudate rather than the rat nucleus accumbens.
Proposed Thalamocortical Decoupling and Acute Sensory Alterations: In a comparative circuit schematic, Ganado et al. (2026) propose salvinorin A–associated thalamocortical decoupling/dissociation; however, direct mechanism-to-phenomenology evidence establishing how KOR activation produces these acute subjective states in humans is lacking (Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). In review-level summaries of controlled human inhalation studies in experienced hallucinogen users, inhaled salvinorin A is reported to induce rapid-onset spatial/temporal distortions, vivid dissociative or out-of-body experiences, and altered somatic awareness within seconds, peaking at approximately 2 minutes and declining by 30 minutes (Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). Notably, while Ganado et al. (2026) describe the typical salvinorin A state as dysphoric—consistent with aversive features emphasized in preclinical and anecdotal accounts—Butelman & Kreek (2015) report no pronounced dysphoria in controlled human laboratory studies limited to experienced hallucinogen users, while noting that it remains unknown whether these findings generalize to other populations.
### Ibogaine: Polypharmacology and GDNF Signaling
Ibogaine acts across multiple receptor networks simultaneously (Ganado et al., 2026):
Receptor Targets: Exhibits polypharmacological affinity across multiple targets—including NMDARs, κ-opioid receptors (KOR), μ-opioid receptors (MOR), 5-HT2A receptors, sodium channels, serotonin and dopamine transporters (SERT/DAT), and α3β4 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (Ganado et al., 2026)—with these interactions reported at the level of target affinity rather than established functional agonism, channel blockade, or transporter inhibition across all sites.
Metabolism & Pharmacodynamics: Ibogaine undergoes O-demethylation predominantly catalyzed by cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) in human liver microsomes to form its primary metabolite, 12-hydroxyibogamine (noribogaine) (Obach et al., 1998). Noribogaine exhibits a long elimination half-life—with a range of dose-group mean half-life estimates of 28–49 hours in a randomized study of 36 healthy, drug-free male volunteers receiving single oral doses of 3, 10, 30, or 60 mg noribogaine or placebo (Glue et al., 2015) and a range of dose-group mean half-life estimates of 24–30 hours in a randomized study of 27 opioid-dependent patients receiving single oral doses of 60, 120, or 180 mg noribogaine or placebo after switching from methadone to morphine (Glue et al., 2016). In preclinical binding and neurochemical studies, noribogaine binds to opioid receptors including MOR and KOR (Pearl et al., 1995) and targets the serotonin transporter (SERT) to elevate serotonin (Mash et al., 1995). Clinically and preclinically, ibogaine has been studied for reducing withdrawal symptoms and interrupting addictive behaviors (Ganado et al., 2026).
Phenomenology: Induces a prolonged altered state (described in the secondary review by Ganado et al., 2026, as prolonged and putatively metabolite-dependent, though exact clinical duration windows—including acute versus post-acute residual effects—are not established here by a cited primary clinical study with specified dose, route, sample size, or direct metabolite-duration evidence and must be distinguished from the prolonged pharmacokinetic elimination of noribogaine; Glue et al., 2015, 2016).
Neuroplasticity & Toxicity: In preclinical rodent models of ethanol consumption, systemic ibogaine increased Glial Cell Line-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF) mRNA in a dissected midbrain region containing the VTA—a regional expression measurement rather than a VTA-only expression assay—whereas separate site-specific functional experiments using intra-VTA ibogaine or GDNF manipulations and anti-GDNF neutralizing antibodies support a functional role of GDNF in the VTA in reducing ethanol self-administration; collectively, these data support GDNF involvement in ibogaine's reduction of ethanol consumption in rodents (He et al., 2005). However, ibogaine inhibits hERG potassium channels in vitro (IC50 = 4 μM in heterologously expressed channels; Koenig et al., 2014) and prolongs the cardiac QT interval, carrying a risk of ventricular arrhythmias including torsades de pointes (Ganado et al., 2026). This risk has motivated research into potentially safer synthetic analogs, such as oxa-iboga derivatives (Ganado et al., 2026).
### Indirect Opioid Recruitment by Classical Psychedelics
5-HT2AR signaling is hypothesized to cross-talk with endogenous opioid networks. According to this hypothesis, activation of 5-HT2A receptors by classical psychedelics may indirectly stimulate the release of endogenous enkephalins and β-endorphins—potentially engaging downstream MOR and δ-opioid receptors (DOR) to contribute to analgesic and mood-elevating effects—although direct empirical evidence for this hypothesis remains limited (Ganado et al., 2026).

## CHAPTER 7: CLINICAL TRANSLATION, SAFETY, AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
### Subjective Experience vs. Pure Neuroplasticity
A central debate in translational neuropsychopharmacology centers on whether the acute subjective trip is strictly required for long-term therapeutic efficacy (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
Schematic Summary (Therapeutic Paradigms): Two competing translational frameworks are under debate: Paradigm A (Non-Hallucinogenic Psychoplastogens) evaluates two separate claims that must be supported by distinct lines of evidence: (1) the receptor/plasticity claim that direct molecular engagement (e.g., via proposed intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement—for which the underlying mechanism of intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning remains unresolved—or contested TrkB pathways) may induce structural neuroplasticity without eliciting hallucinogenic-like behavioral markers (such as the rodent head-twitch response [HTR]) or, by translational hypothesis, a subjective hallucinogenic experience in humans (Vargas et al., 2023; Moliner et al., 2023; Banushi & Polito, 2023); and (2) the separate electrophysiological claim that such plasticity occurs without acute cortical desynchronization—which is not established by an absence of HTR or a proposed non-hallucinogenic profile, as the cited receptor and plasticity studies do not provide the comparative cortical electrophysiological measurements needed to test cortical desynchronization; Paradigm B (Experience-Dependent Restructuring / REBUS) proposes that acute 5-HT2AR-mediated cortical dysregulation relaxes (loosens) rigid high-level priors and sensitizes them to bottom-up information flow, enabling possible revision of entrenched priors and theoretical Bayesian model reduction (BMR) when guided by appropriate intention, care provision, and context—without establishing reportable ego dissolution as a necessary condition for all therapeutically relevant belief revision (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
The Non-Hallucinogenic Hypothesis: Drug developers are investigating biased ligands and "psychoplastogens" (e.g., non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A agonists targeting proposed intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement—with intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning unresolved—or proposed TrkB-targeting ligands) with the goal of promoting structural neuroplasticity without producing hallucinogenic effects. Here, two claims must be kept distinct: (1) preclinical receptor and plasticity experiments test whether candidate ligands promote cellular plasticity endpoints (such as dendritic branching, spine density, or spinogenesis) without eliciting hallucinogenic-like behavioral markers such as rodent HTR—a translational hypothesis for avoiding subjective hallucinations in humans (Vargas et al., 2023; Moliner et al., 2023; Banushi & Polito, 2023); and (2) whether such compounds also avoid acute cortical desynchronization is a separate electrophysiological question that cannot be inferred from an absence of HTR or a proposed non-hallucinogenic profile, because the cited receptor and plasticity studies do not provide the comparative electrophysiological experiments required to establish absence of cortical desynchronization.
The Experience-Dependent Hypothesis: The theoretical REBUS model proposes that psychedelics loosen (relax) the weighting of rigid high-level priors and sensitize them to liberated bottom-up information flow, thereby supporting possible revision of entrenched priors—with intention, care provision, and therapeutic context playing a central role in shaping outcomes (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Importantly, REBUS does not establish that reportable ego dissolution is a necessary condition for all therapeutically relevant belief revision; framing acute subjective ego dissolution as strictly "necessary" is a stronger interpretive claim beyond what the model demonstrates, rather than a proven requirement (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Within this theoretical model, acute subjective experiences are instead proposed as a conscious counterpart that can accompany prior relaxation and theoretical Bayesian model reduction.
### Safety, Risk Management, and Set and Setting
Set and Setting: Non-pharmacological contextual factors—including patient psychological state ("set") and therapeutic environment ("setting")—are widely thought to influence acute experiences and clinical outcomes (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Within the REBUS theoretical framework, contextual variables are hypothesized to modulate baseline neural network dynamics and shape how precision-weighted priors relax under acute drug action (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019).
Biased Ligand Drug Discovery: Future drug development relies on structural modeling of GPCR and ion channel complexes to engineer molecules optimized for selective cell-type recruitment, tailored signaling profiles, and reduced cardiac or psychotomimetic risks (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
