CHAPTER 2: CLASSICAL SEROTONERGIC PSYCHEDELICS — RECEPTOR PHARMACOLOGY AND INTRACELLULAR SIGNALING

Centrality of the 5-HT2A Receptor

The primary mechanism underlying the hallucinogenic properties of classical psychedelics is partial or full agonism at the serotonin 2A receptor (5-HT2AR), a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Banushi & Polito, 2023). While 5-HT2AR binding affinity is broadly associated with human hallucinogenic potency—though receptor binding affinity alone does not determine potency—human challenge studies using pre-administration of the 5-HT2AR antagonist ketanserin provide compound-specific evidence rather than a single uniform demonstration across agents; notably, ketanserin is not exclusively selective for 5-HT2AR (exhibiting 5-HT2A/2C antagonism; Knight et al., 2004), whereas more selective 5-HT2AR antagonists such as M100907 (MDL 100,907, exhibiting greater than approximately 100-fold selectivity for 5-HT2A over other measured receptors—including 5-HT2C—in the cited binding panels, though exact fold-selectivity values depend on the specific assay panel used) exist (Kehne et al., 1996; Knight et al., 2004). Specifically, Preller et al. (2019) directly tested LSD in humans (showing ketanserin blockade of acute subjective effects and 5-HT2AR-dependent thalamus–PCC effective connectivity changes, though not all connectivity alterations); Vollenweider et al. (1998) evaluated ketanserin pretreatment against psilocybin in healthy human volunteers, demonstrating dose-dependent blockade of psilocybin-induced effects; and human ketanserin evidence relating to DMT involves ayahuasca—a brew combining DMT with β-carboline MAO inhibitors rather than pure DMT alone—in which oral ketanserin (40 mg; n = 12) inhibited EEG alpha modifications and attenuated rather than completely abolished acute subjective effects of ayahuasca (0.75 mg DMT/kg) (Valle et al., 2016). 5-HT2A receptors are expressed most densely on the apical dendrites of layer 5 pyramidal neurons within the neocortex, particularly within high-level association areas (such as the default mode network nodes) and primary/extrastriate visual cortices (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Banushi & Polito, 2023). Schematic Summary (5-HT2AR Intracellular Signaling): Ligand binding at 5-HT2AR engages two reviewed intracellular signaling branches (Banushi & Polito, 2023): (1) the canonical Gq/11 pathway activating PLCβ (correcting Banushi & Polito’s erroneous reference to PLCγ) to cleave PIP2 into IP3 (mobilizing intracellular Ca2+) and DAG (activating PKC), converging on ERK1/2 and CREB modulation; and (2) PI3K/AKT signaling. ### Functional Selectivity and Biased Agonism Non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A agonists can stimulate the receptor without reliably inducing hallucinogenic behavioral outputs (e.g., the head-twitch response [HTR] in rodents), whereas the case of serotonin is distinct and conditional: peripheral/systemic serotonin administration is not equivalent to delivering serotonin into the brain or to intracellular 5-HT2AR pools, and serotonin or serotonin-releasing manipulations can induce HTR under appropriate conditions—indeed, Vargas et al. (2023) reported HTR following a serotonin-releasing agent in mice engineered to express the serotonin transporter (SERT) in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Functional selectivity (biased agonism) has been proposed as one model that may explain ligand divergence at 5-HT2AR, alongside location bias, pharmacokinetics, and signaling kinetics (Banushi & Polito, 2023; Vargas et al., 2023), while Vargas et al. (2023) note that traditional biased agonism alone does not explain serotonin’s growth phenotype. Upon ligand binding, GPCRs adopt distinct active conformations that preferentially recruit specific intracellular transducers: Gq/11 Activation: Canonical binding triggers Gq/11-mediated activation of Phospholipase C beta (PLCβ)—noting that Banushi & Polito (2023) erroneously name PLCγ here, whereas PLCβ is the canonical Gq/11 effector—leading to the cleavage of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). IP3 mobilizes intracellular calcium (Ca2+) from the endoplasmic reticulum, while DAG activates Protein Kinase C (PKC), downstream Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK1/2), and Cyclic AMP Response Element-Binding Protein (CREB) (Banushi & Polito, 2023). PI3K/AKT Signaling: In addition to canonical Gq/11 signaling, 5-HT2AR activation engages Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and AKT signaling cascades (Banushi & Polito, 2023). At the local network level, 5-HT2AR activation on layer 5 pyramidal neurons promotes asynchronous glutamate release from thalamocortical and corticocortical terminals, generating spike-field decoherence and altering local recurrent excitation (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). ### Location Bias: Intracellular 5-HT2A Receptors A critical paradox in neuropharmacology is why endogenous serotonin does not trigger the same structural plastic changes as lipophilic psychedelics despite possessing high affinity for 5-HT2AR. In preclinical neuronal models, Vargas et al. (2023) proposed that location bias accounts for this divergence in structural neuroplasticity. A substantial pool of 5-HT2A receptors resides inside cortical neurons within the Golgi apparatus and endosomal membranes, whereas embryonic rat cortical neurons do not naturally express SERT at appreciable levels (Vargas et al., 2023). To test whether intracellular access enables serotonin-driven plasticity, Vargas et al. (2023) electroporated embryonic rat cortical neurons with a SERT construct under a CaMKII promoter, generating engineered SERT-positive alongside SERT-negative neurons. Serotonin promoted dendritic growth in these engineered SERT-positive neurons—an effect on the dendrite-growth/Sholl endpoint (Figure 4D) that was blocked by citalopram or ketanserin—whereas unmodified/SERT-negative neurons did not show this response; spine growth in SERT-positive neurons was assessed separately (Figure 4E–F) rather than through the same direct inhibitor comparison (Vargas et al., 2023). Similarly, their in vivo experiments relied on viral SERT expression in the mouse mPFC followed by a serotonin-releasing agent, and therefore do not demonstrate that normal endogenous serotonin accesses intracellular 5-HT2AR pools in unmodified cortical pyramidal neurons (Vargas et al., 2023). Conversely, more lipophilic psychedelic molecules (such as DMT, psilocin, and LSD) more readily cross the plasma membrane to engage intracellular 5-HT2AR populations (Vargas et al., 2023). Across the preclinical models tested by Vargas et al. (2023), the directly measured endpoints encompassed dendritic branching, dendritic spine density, and electrophysiological changes (including spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents [sEPSCs])—collectively interpreted cautiously as structural neuroplasticity—but receptor dependence and intracellular access were demonstrated in distinct experiments: 5-HT2AR knockout prevented the sustained sEPSC changes after 5-MeO-DMT in mice (demonstrating receptor dependence rather than specifically proving intracellular receptor necessity; Supplementary Figure S1C); separate membrane-permeability experiments addressed intracellular access for structural endpoints such as dendritic branching and spine density (Figures 3–5). Furthermore, as Vargas et al. (2023) note in their Discussion, their experiments support intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement but do not distinguish intracellular signaling from alternative hypotheses such as pharmacological chaperoning; thus, the underlying mechanism (intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning) remains unresolved, and whether intracellular 5-HT2AR engagement is sufficient to explain neuroplasticity in humans has not been demonstrated (Vargas et al., 2023). ### Direct TrkB Binding and Neuroplasticity Beyond traditional GPCR activation, direct engagement of neurotrophin signaling pathways has also been proposed, although this mechanism remains contested. In preclinical molecular and cellular models, Moliner et al. (2023) reported that LSD and psilocin bind directly to Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), the primary receptor for Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) (Moliner et al., 2023). In that study, LSD and psilocin were reported to bind to the dimeric transmembrane domain of TrkB with high binding affinity—measured in vitro using immunoprecipitated TrkB expressed in a heterologous HEK293T cell system (a heterologous in vitro receptor preparation rather than native human brain tissue) and described as an approximate, rounded ~1,000-fold higher affinity relative to comparator antidepressants (for example, psilocin Ki = 6.73 nM versus fluoxetine Ki = 4.77 μM [~700-fold] and ketamine Ki = 20.82 μM [~3,100-fold]), rather than an exact 1,000-fold measurement or 1,000-fold signal amplification (Moliner et al., 2023). According to Moliner et al. (2023), this binding stabilizes the active dimeric conformation of TrkB, facilitating endogenous BDNF signaling, dendritic arborization, and spinogenesis (Moliner et al., 2023). In their binding-site characterization experiments, both the Y433F and V437A point mutations in the TrkB transmembrane domain markedly reduced binding, while a separate manipulation replacing the TrkB transmembrane domain with the TrkA domain resulted in loss of binding in radioligand assays; however, the dissociation between neuroplasticity and behavior in mice—loss of neuroplastic effects without preventing the 5-HT2AR-mediated head-twitch response, a behavioral marker rather than a direct measure of hallucinogenic subjective effects—was specifically established with the Y433F system, including heterozygous Y433F mice, which the authors interpreted as evidence for a 5-HT2AR-independent dissociation between this behavioral marker and sustained neuroplasticity via BDNF-dependent allosteric potentiation (Moliner et al., 2023). However, these findings have been challenged by subsequent profiling: in a comprehensive study profiling 41 psychedelics across 318 human GPCRs, Jain et al. (2025) screened LSD against over 450 human kinases (including the intracellular kinase domain of TrkB) and found no significant interaction, and in a functional TrkB AP1-luciferase reporter assay in TrkB-transfected cells specifically comparing LSD and psilocin, neither compound affected BDNF-driven TrkB reporter activation (assessing downstream transcriptional AP1-luciferase signaling under transfected-cell conditions rather than directly probing transmembrane-domain binding; Jain et al., 2025). While the kinase-binding screen does not directly test Moliner et al.’s (2023) proposed transmembrane binding site and therefore does not by itself refute binding at that site, the failure to detect modulation of BDNF-driven TrkB activity in the AP1-luciferase reporter assay directly bears on Moliner et al.’s (2023) explicitly proposed functional mechanism of BDNF-dependent allosteric potentiation; overall, the assays differ, the evidence remains conflicting and inconclusive, and direct TrkB engagement by psychedelics remains an ongoing debate (Moliner et al., 2023; Jain et al., 2025). Moreover, Moliner et al.’s (2023) proposal of 5-HT2AR-independent plasticity stands in direct tension with Vargas et al. (2023), who reported that intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement (with intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning unresolved) drives structural neuroplasticity in their preclinical models, rather than representing cleanly complementary mechanisms. ### Secondary Receptor Targets Classical psychedelics interact with a broad spectrum of secondary targets: 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C Receptors: In addition to 5-HT2AR, classical psychedelics interact with secondary serotonin receptor subtypes, including 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C receptors (Banushi & Polito, 2023). Dopamine D2 Receptors: LSD displays high affinity for dopamine D2 receptors; in rat behavioral models, it produces a characteristically biphasic behavioral effect wherein late-stage behavioral dynamics are mediated by D2R engagement (Banushi & Polito, 2023). Trace Amine-Associated Receptor 1 (TAAR1): Some tryptamines interact with TAAR1 in experimental assays; however, receptor activity observed in experimental systems must be distinguished from established in vivo functional consequences, and the contribution of this interaction to DMT’s psychedelic effects is unresolved (Su et al., 2009; Banushi & Polito, 2023). Sigma-1 Receptors (σ1R): DMT acts as a ligand at σ1R, localized at the mitochondrion-associated endoplasmic reticulum membrane (MAM), with an in vitro binding affinity described as “about 14 μM” in Su et al. (2009, reviewing Fontanilla et al., 2009) and cited as “14 μM” in the theoretical model in the caption of Figure 3 of Banushi & Polito (2023); importantly, this value reflects in vitro binding affinity rather than an endogenous concentration in the brain. Within the theoretical framework reviewed by Su et al. (2009) and Banushi & Polito (2023), σ1R actions are separated into two distinct concentration branches: (a) at concentrations near binding affinity, proposed σ1R/BiP dissociation and IP3-receptor chaperoning, along with proposed modulation of endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer and ATP production; and (b) at higher concentrations, illustrated by ~100 μM DMT, proposed receptor redistribution alongside experimentally observed in vitro ion-channel inhibition (Fontanilla et al., 2009). Crucially, proposed mechanisms must be distinguished from experimentally observed findings: the DMT chaperone/transport scheme (σ1R/BiP dissociation, IP3-receptor chaperoning, endoplasmic reticulum–mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, ATP production, and proposed cellular protection against oxidative stress) is a hypothesis extrapolated from general σ1R biology and other σ1R ligands, whereas the in vitro ion-channel experiments with DMT do not demonstrate that those same preparations exhibit DMT-induced mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, increased ATP production, or protection against oxidative stress—and none of these proposed pathways has been established in the human brain (Fontanilla et al., 2009; Su et al., 2009; Banushi & Polito, 2023).
