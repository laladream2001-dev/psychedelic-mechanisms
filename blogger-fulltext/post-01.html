Hallucinogenic Mechanisms of Psychedelics: From Molecular Pharmacology to Predictive Processing

A Comprehensive Monograph on Classical Serotonergic Psychedelics, Dissociative NMDA Antagonists, and Atypical Opioid Hallucinogens

PREFACE & SCOPE

The scientific understanding of altered states of consciousness induced by psychoactive substances has undergone a paradigm shift over the past two decades. Historically categorized under broad, descriptive terms, modern neuropharmacology and computational neuroscience now differentiate these agents into distinct functional and mechanistically defined classes. This monograph presents a multi-level synthesis spanning molecular pharmacology, intracellular signaling cascades, structural and functional neuroplasticity, large-scale neural network dynamics, and theoretical/computational models of brain function (specifically hierarchical predictive processing and active inference). The scope encompasses three primary classes of hallucinogenic and psychoactive compounds: Classical Serotonergic Psychedelics: Tryptamines (e.g., psilocybin, DMT), ergolines (e.g., LSD), and phenethylamines (e.g., mescaline, 2C-B, 25I-NBOMe) acting principally via 5-HT2A receptor agonism. Dissociative Anesthetics: Use-dependent, uncompetitive N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) open-channel blockers (e.g., ketamine, PCP). Atypical Hallucinogens: Kappa-opioid receptor (KOR) agonists (e.g., salvinorin A) and polypharmacological indole alkaloids (e.g., ibogaine). CHAPTER 1: TAXONOMY AND CHEMICAL ARCHITECTURE OF HALLUCINOGENS

The academic investigation of hallucinogenic agents has evolved significantly since psychiatrist Humphry Osmond coined the term “psychedelic” (mind-manifesting) in 1956 (Osmond, 1957), and since Albert Hofmann’s synthesis of lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) in 1938, followed by his accidental exposure on 16 April 1943 and intentional use on 19 April 1943 (as reviewed in the secondary historical account by McCorvy & Roth, 2015). Early clinical interest waned following mid-20th-century regulatory restrictions, but contemporary high-resolution neuroimaging, structural biology, and molecular signaling assays have catalyzed a renaissance in translational psychedelic science (Banushi & Polito, 2023). Modern chemical taxonomy classifies hallucinogens based on primary molecular scaffolds, pharmacophores, and core target binding profiles. Schematic Summary (Chemical Classification): Hallucinogenic agents are organized into three primary classes: (1) classical serotonergic psychedelics—tryptamines (psilocybin, DMT), ergolines (LSD), and phenethylamines (mescaline, 25I-NBOMe); (2) classical dissociative anesthetics acting as use-dependent, uncompetitive NMDAR open-channel blockers (ketamine, PCP); and (3) atypical hallucinogens—neoclerodane diterpenes (salvinorin A at KOR) and polypharmacological indole alkaloids (ibogaine). ### Classical Serotonergic Psychedelics Classical psychedelics share structural homology with the endogenous neurotransmitter serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) and belong to three principal chemical classes: Tryptamines: Characterized by a core indole ring connected to an amino group via a two-carbon ethyl chain. Representative agents include psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, a prodrug rapidly dephosphorylated in vivo to the active metabolite psilocin), N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT) (Banushi & Polito, 2023). Ergolines: Complex tetracyclic compounds built on a rigid tetracyclic ergoline scaffold containing both indole and phenethylamine motifs. Lysergamides represent a subclass of ergolines defined by a lysergic acid amide functionality rather than defining the ergoline class as a whole (McCorvy & Roth, 2015). LSD is the canonical psychedelic lysergamide (an ergoline derivative), exhibiting high affinity across serotonin, dopamine, and adrenergic receptor subtypes (Banushi & Polito, 2023). Phenethylamines: Composed of a benzene ring linked to an amino group by an ethyl chain. Key members include naturally occurring mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) and synthetic modifications such as 2C-B (4-bromo-2,5-dimethoxyphenethylamine), DOI (4-iodo-2,5-dimethoxyamphetamine), DOM, and the high-affinity N-(2-methoxybenzyl) derivatives such as 25I-NBOMe (Banushi & Polito, 2023; Ganado et al., 2026). ### Dissociative Anesthetics Classical dissociative anesthetics such as ketamine (specifically its (S)- and (R)-enantiomers) and phencyclidine (PCP) disrupt sensory transmission and conscious awareness primarily through use-dependent, uncompetitive open-channel blockade of the ionotropic N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) channel complex (MacDonald et al., 1991; Marguilho et al., 2023). Although gaseous nitrous oxide (N2O) is also an inhalational anesthetic that inhibits postsynaptic NMDAR function, electrophysiological evidence argues against classifying it as a classical ketamine-like, use-dependent open-channel blocker: N2O inhibition develops rapidly without altering NMDAR excitatory current decay kinetics and appears independent of channel opening (Mennerick et al., 1998). ### Atypical Hallucinogens Compounds outside classical serotonergic or NMDAR-antagonist categories produce distinct hallucinogenic profiles: Salvinorin A: A non-nitrogenous neoclerodane diterpene isolated from Salvia divinorum. Unlike virtually all other known hallucinogens, it lacks an amine nitrogen and acts as a selective agonist at the κ-opioid receptor (KOR) relative to other major opioid receptor subtypes and 5-HT2AR within screened binding panels (Butelman & Kreek, 2015). Ibogaine: A monoterpene indole alkaloid extracted from Tabernanthe iboga. Ibogaine features a polypharmacological profile, interacting with NMDARs, KORs, μ-opioid receptors (MOR), 5-HT receptors, and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α3β4 nAChR) (Ganado et al., 2026).
