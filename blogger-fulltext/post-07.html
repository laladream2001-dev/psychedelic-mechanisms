CHAPTER 7: CLINICAL TRANSLATION, SAFETY, AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Subjective Experience vs. Pure Neuroplasticity

A central debate in translational neuropsychopharmacology centers on whether the acute subjective trip is strictly required for long-term therapeutic efficacy (Banushi & Polito, 2023). Schematic Summary (Therapeutic Paradigms): Two competing translational frameworks are under debate: Paradigm A (Non-Hallucinogenic Psychoplastogens) evaluates two separate claims that must be supported by distinct lines of evidence: (1) the receptor/plasticity claim that direct molecular engagement (e.g., via proposed intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement—for which the underlying mechanism of intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning remains unresolved—or contested TrkB pathways) may induce structural neuroplasticity without eliciting hallucinogenic-like behavioral markers (such as the rodent head-twitch response [HTR]) or, by translational hypothesis, a subjective hallucinogenic experience in humans (Vargas et al., 2023; Moliner et al., 2023; Banushi & Polito, 2023); and (2) the separate electrophysiological claim that such plasticity occurs without acute cortical desynchronization—which is not established by an absence of HTR or a proposed non-hallucinogenic profile, as the cited receptor and plasticity studies do not provide the comparative cortical electrophysiological measurements needed to test cortical desynchronization; Paradigm B (Experience-Dependent Restructuring / REBUS) proposes that acute 5-HT2AR-mediated cortical dysregulation relaxes (loosens) rigid high-level priors and sensitizes them to bottom-up information flow, enabling possible revision of entrenched priors and theoretical Bayesian model reduction (BMR) when guided by appropriate intention, care provision, and context—without establishing reportable ego dissolution as a necessary condition for all therapeutically relevant belief revision (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). The Non-Hallucinogenic Hypothesis: Drug developers are investigating biased ligands and “psychoplastogens” (e.g., non-hallucinogenic 5-HT2A agonists targeting proposed intracellular 5-HT2AR access/engagement—with intracellular signaling vs. pharmacological chaperoning unresolved—or proposed TrkB-targeting ligands) with the goal of promoting structural neuroplasticity without producing hallucinogenic effects. Here, two claims must be kept distinct: (1) preclinical receptor and plasticity experiments test whether candidate ligands promote cellular plasticity endpoints (such as dendritic branching, spine density, or spinogenesis) without eliciting hallucinogenic-like behavioral markers such as rodent HTR—a translational hypothesis for avoiding subjective hallucinations in humans (Vargas et al., 2023; Moliner et al., 2023; Banushi & Polito, 2023); and (2) whether such compounds also avoid acute cortical desynchronization is a separate electrophysiological question that cannot be inferred from an absence of HTR or a proposed non-hallucinogenic profile, because the cited receptor and plasticity studies do not provide the comparative electrophysiological experiments required to establish absence of cortical desynchronization. The Experience-Dependent Hypothesis: The theoretical REBUS model proposes that psychedelics loosen (relax) the weighting of rigid high-level priors and sensitize them to liberated bottom-up information flow, thereby supporting possible revision of entrenched priors—with intention, care provision, and therapeutic context playing a central role in shaping outcomes (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Importantly, REBUS does not establish that reportable ego dissolution is a necessary condition for all therapeutically relevant belief revision; framing acute subjective ego dissolution as strictly “necessary” is a stronger interpretive claim beyond what the model demonstrates, rather than a proven requirement (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Within this theoretical model, acute subjective experiences are instead proposed as a conscious counterpart that can accompany prior relaxation and theoretical Bayesian model reduction. ### Safety, Risk Management, and Set and Setting Set and Setting: Non-pharmacological contextual factors—including patient psychological state (“set”) and therapeutic environment (“setting”)—are widely thought to influence acute experiences and clinical outcomes (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Within the REBUS theoretical framework, contextual variables are hypothesized to modulate baseline neural network dynamics and shape how precision-weighted priors relax under acute drug action (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). Biased Ligand Drug Discovery: Future drug development relies on structural modeling of GPCR and ion channel complexes to engineer molecules optimized for selective cell-type recruitment, tailored signaling profiles, and reduced cardiac or psychotomimetic risks (Banushi & Polito, 2023).
