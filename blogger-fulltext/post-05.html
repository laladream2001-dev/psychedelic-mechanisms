CHAPTER 5: ATYPICAL HALLUCINOGENS — KAPPA-OPIOID AGONISM (SALVINORIN A) AND POLYPHARMACOLOGY (IBOGAINE)

Salvinorin A and Kappa-Opioid Receptor (KOR) Dynamics

Salvinorin A, isolated from Salvia divinorum, is a neoclerodane diterpene lacking a basic nitrogen atom (Butelman & Kreek, 2015). It acts as a potent, selective agonist at the κ-opioid receptor (KOR) (e.g., G-protein activation EC50 = 4.73 nM at mouse KOR; White et al., 2015), with virtually no affinity for 5-HT2AR and selectivity bounded by the receptor panels screened (Butelman & Kreek, 2015). Schematic Summary (Salvinorin A KOR Signaling): KOR engagement by salvinorin A activates the canonical Gi/o pathway—inhibiting adenylyl cyclase, reducing intracellular cAMP, opening GIRK channels, and inhibiting voltage-gated Ca2+ channels to promote neuronal hyperpolarization (Ganado et al., 2026)—and also directly recruits β-arrestin and drives strong receptor internalization (White et al., 2014; White et al., 2015). A balanced (unbiased) KOR agonist engages both measured G-protein and arrestin pathways roughly equivalently within the tested assay system; accordingly, primary profiling contradicts Ganado et al.’s (2026) claim of pronounced β-arrestin bias, not the existence of β-arrestin recruitment itself, while specific downstream p38 MAPK/JNK effects and proposed links to transient plasticity or phenomenology remain separate questions (White et al., 2014; White et al., 2015; Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). At the cellular level, KOR activation by Salvinorin A engages two primary pathways: Gi/o Signaling: Inhibits adenylyl cyclase, reducing intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels, opening G protein-coupled inwardly rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels, and inhibiting voltage-gated Ca2+ channels. This hyperpolarizes target neurons and inhibits neurotransmitter release (Ganado et al., 2026). β-Arrestin Recruitment, Relative Pathway Bias, and Downstream Signaling: Interpreting non-G-protein signaling by salvinorin A requires separating four distinct levels of evidence: (a) Demonstrated arrestin recruitment: A balanced (unbiased) agonist denotes roughly equivalent engagement of the measured pathways within a given assay system—it does not mean an absence of β-arrestin recruitment. Primary pharmacological assays directly measure β-arrestin recruitment by salvinorin A (e.g., arrestin mobilization EC50 = 10.5 nM alongside G-protein activation EC50 = 4.73 nM at mouse KOR) together with strong KOR internalization (White et al., 2014; White et al., 2015). (b) Relative pathway bias: These primary experiments contradict Ganado et al.’s (2026) characterization of salvinorin A as exhibiting pronounced β-arrestin bias, not the existence of arrestin recruitment itself. In White et al.’s (2015) RB-64 comparison analysis, salvinorin A has a bias factor of 1 because values are normalized to it as the reference ligand within that assay system—which does not establish a universal absence of bias across all pathways, cell types, species, and assay time points, as White et al. (2015) explicitly discuss assay and system limitations—whereas the salvinorin A derivative RB-64 exhibits pronounced G-protein bias at mouse KOR (bias factor = 96 toward G protein; White et al., 2015), consistent with Butelman & Kreek (2015, citing White et al., 2014). (c) Evidence for specific p38 MAPK / JNK effects: Downstream p38 Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) and c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK) activation and rapid receptor desensitization are attributed to KOR β-arrestin-2 signaling in the review by Ganado et al. (2026), but are distinct from—and not demonstrated as a salvinorin A–specific biased signature by—White et al.’s (2014, 2015) arrestin-recruitment and internalization assays. (d) Proposed links to plasticity and phenomenology: Proposed connections between these intracellular cascades and transient functional plasticity or dissociative phenomenology remain interpretive; in contrast to classical serotonergic psychedelics, Ganado et al. (2026) contrast salvinorin A’s transient functional plasticity with sustained structural spinogenesis, though the cited review provides no direct dendritic-spine imaging evidence for salvinorin A. ### Circuit-Level Mechanisms of Salvinorin A VTA-Nucleus Accumbens Suppression: KORs are heavily expressed on presynaptic dopaminergic terminals originating in the VTA and projecting to the nucleus accumbens (Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). As summarized in Ganado et al. (2026), rat nucleus accumbens microdialysis studies report a reduction in extracellular dopamine (dialysate) following systemic salvinorin A, a preclinical observation associated with conditioned place aversion in animal models—though Butelman & Kreek (2015) also raise the possibility of increased dopamine transporter (DAT) activity rather than solely reduced dopamine release. However, dopaminergic and behavioral reinforcement responses vary substantially by dose, route, species, and brain region—indicating that preclinical reinforcement evidence is limited and inconsistent, and route- and dose-dependent, rather than showing an absence of rewarding effects: in rats, Braida et al. (2008) reported conditioned place preference and intracerebroventricular self-administration at certain doses (alongside low-dose dopamine increases), and Serra et al. (2015) found increased nucleus accumbens extracellular dopamine (dialysate) without stable intravenous self-administration at the tested doses, whereas Zhang et al. (2005) measured extracellular dopamine (dialysate) reductions in the mouse caudate rather than the rat nucleus accumbens. Proposed Thalamocortical Decoupling and Acute Sensory Alterations: In a comparative circuit schematic, Ganado et al. (2026) propose salvinorin A–associated thalamocortical decoupling/dissociation; however, direct mechanism-to-phenomenology evidence establishing how KOR activation produces these acute subjective states in humans is lacking (Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). In review-level summaries of controlled human inhalation studies in experienced hallucinogen users, inhaled salvinorin A is reported to induce rapid-onset spatial/temporal distortions, vivid dissociative or out-of-body experiences, and altered somatic awareness within seconds, peaking at approximately 2 minutes and declining by 30 minutes (Butelman & Kreek, 2015; Ganado et al., 2026). Notably, while Ganado et al. (2026) describe the typical salvinorin A state as dysphoric—consistent with aversive features emphasized in preclinical and anecdotal accounts—Butelman & Kreek (2015) report no pronounced dysphoria in controlled human laboratory studies limited to experienced hallucinogen users, while noting that it remains unknown whether these findings generalize to other populations. ### Ibogaine: Polypharmacology and GDNF Signaling Ibogaine acts across multiple receptor networks simultaneously (Ganado et al., 2026): Receptor Targets: Exhibits polypharmacological affinity across multiple targets—including NMDARs, κ-opioid receptors (KOR), μ-opioid receptors (MOR), 5-HT2A receptors, sodium channels, serotonin and dopamine transporters (SERT/DAT), and α3β4 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (Ganado et al., 2026)—with these interactions reported at the level of target affinity rather than established functional agonism, channel blockade, or transporter inhibition across all sites. Metabolism & Pharmacodynamics: Ibogaine undergoes O-demethylation predominantly catalyzed by cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) in human liver microsomes to form its primary metabolite, 12-hydroxyibogamine (noribogaine) (Obach et al., 1998). Noribogaine exhibits a long elimination half-life—with a range of dose-group mean half-life estimates of 28–49 hours in a randomized study of 36 healthy, drug-free male volunteers receiving single oral doses of 3, 10, 30, or 60 mg noribogaine or placebo (Glue et al., 2015) and a range of dose-group mean half-life estimates of 24–30 hours in a randomized study of 27 opioid-dependent patients receiving single oral doses of 60, 120, or 180 mg noribogaine or placebo after switching from methadone to morphine (Glue et al., 2016). In preclinical binding and neurochemical studies, noribogaine binds to opioid receptors including MOR and KOR (Pearl et al., 1995) and targets the serotonin transporter (SERT) to elevate serotonin (Mash et al., 1995). Clinically and preclinically, ibogaine has been studied for reducing withdrawal symptoms and interrupting addictive behaviors (Ganado et al., 2026). Phenomenology: Induces a prolonged altered state (described in the secondary review by Ganado et al., 2026, as prolonged and putatively metabolite-dependent, though exact clinical duration windows—including acute versus post-acute residual effects—are not established here by a cited primary clinical study with specified dose, route, sample size, or direct metabolite-duration evidence and must be distinguished from the prolonged pharmacokinetic elimination of noribogaine; Glue et al., 2015, 2016). Neuroplasticity & Toxicity: In preclinical rodent models of ethanol consumption, systemic ibogaine increased Glial Cell Line-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF) mRNA in a dissected midbrain region containing the VTA—a regional expression measurement rather than a VTA-only expression assay—whereas separate site-specific functional experiments using intra-VTA ibogaine or GDNF manipulations and anti-GDNF neutralizing antibodies support a functional role of GDNF in the VTA in reducing ethanol self-administration; collectively, these data support GDNF involvement in ibogaine’s reduction of ethanol consumption in rodents (He et al., 2005). However, ibogaine inhibits hERG potassium channels in vitro (IC50 = 4 μM in heterologously expressed channels; Koenig et al., 2014) and prolongs the cardiac QT interval, carrying a risk of ventricular arrhythmias including torsades de pointes (Ganado et al., 2026). This risk has motivated research into potentially safer synthetic analogs, such as oxa-iboga derivatives (Ganado et al., 2026). ### Indirect Opioid Recruitment by Classical Psychedelics 5-HT2AR signaling is hypothesized to cross-talk with endogenous opioid networks. According to this hypothesis, activation of 5-HT2A receptors by classical psychedelics may indirectly stimulate the release of endogenous enkephalins and β-endorphins—potentially engaging downstream MOR and δ-opioid receptors (DOR) to contribute to analgesic and mood-elevating effects—although direct empirical evidence for this hypothesis remains limited (Ganado et al., 2026).
